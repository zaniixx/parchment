I started a "second brain" this week: an Obsidian vault where everything I read, watch and think about gets written down and linked together. Within a day I'd found an objection to the whole idea, and it's about 2,400 years old.

## The complaint

In Plato's *Phaedrus*, Socrates tells the story of Theuth, the Egyptian god who invented writing. Theuth presents it to King Thamus as something that will "make the Egyptians wiser and give them better memories". The king isn't convinced:

> This discovery of yours will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls, because they will not use their memories; they will trust to the external written characters and not remember of themselves.

Writing, Thamus says, is "an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence". It gives people the appearance of wisdom without the real thing.

The irony is hard to miss: we only know Socrates made this argument because Plato wrote it down.

## Was he right?

Partly. In 2011, Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu and Daniel Wegner published a set of experiments called "Google Effects on Memory". People who believed a computer would save what they'd typed remembered less of it. But they were often better at remembering *which folder* it was saved in than the information itself. Memory didn't vanish. It changed jobs, from holding the fact to holding the address.

Whether that's a loss or a fair trade depends on one thing: can you get back to the address when you need it?

## The other side

In 1998 the philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers published "The Extended Mind". Their thought experiment: Inga hears about an exhibition, remembers that the museum is on 53rd Street, and goes. Otto, who has Alzheimer's, looks the address up in the notebook he carries everywhere, and goes. The notebook, they argue, is doing for Otto exactly what memory does for Inga, so it's fair to count it as part of his mind.

If they're right, a well-kept notebook isn't a crutch. It's more mind.

## Where I've landed

Thamus's warning is true of notes you write once and never touch again. That isn't a second brain, it's a filing cabinet. The fix is to keep coming back: link notes to each other, revisit them, and use spaced repetition for the things you actually want in your head.
